Production Heritage • West Texas • Arizona Desert

Born in Hereford, Texas.
Built in the Arizona Desert.

I was born in Hereford, Texas — the "Town Without a Toothache" — where cattle in the city limits outnumbered people 70 to 1, and feedlots, dairies, and rodeo arenas defined the horizon. I grew up balanced between a teacher's classroom and a working cowboy's pasture: my mother taught school during the week, while my father spent weekends team roping, tripping steers, and riding bareback horses. Out on the open plains of West Texas, understanding animal dynamics, physical grit, and the cowboy code was everyday reality.

In the '80s we relocated to Rocky, Oklahoma, a small farming and ranching town in Tornado Alley where I learned what it takes to weather hard storms and show up without excuses. Later, Queen Creek, Arizona became home back when it was still red dirt roads, cotton fields, and working spreads. Competing in High School and College Rodeos in Team Roping and Bulldogging taught me that whether in the arena or in film financing: you either commit and execute, or you taste dirt.

I built my production career around three non-negotiables: faith, family, and your word. Everything else — the screenplays, the feature packaging, and physical logistics — flows directly out of that foundation.

Practical Horsemanship • Livestock Logistics • Western Authenticity

Authentic Western Horsemanship & Range Logistics

You can't manufacture authentic frontier horsemanship on a studio lot. From pasture gathering across vast Sandhills spreads to laying loops in the Arizona brush and doctoring stock in rough washes, real range expertise is what safeguards livestock logistics and period accuracy on set.

The Open Range • Pasture Gathering

Big Pastures & Heavy Skies — Gathering the Herds

True frontier distance reveals itself when you’re horseback behind several hundred head of black cattle stretching across the horizon. Whether pushing herds across the sprawling Sandhills of Nebraska or navigating high desert pastures, this is where practical stockmanship begins: reading flight zones, matching pace, and holding stock safely together through shifting weather.

Range Companions • The Daily Grind

Me, My Horse & Demon — Trust on the Trot

A seasoned mount gives you your legs, but a gritty cattle dog gives you your reach. Out on the range, Demon works thick mesquite, keeps strays pointed true, and scouts perimeter lines before the sun climbs high. Out here, a cowboy's day comes down to the mount he saddles and the stock dog at his stirrup.

Hand Mechanics • Desert Stockmanship

Building the Loop — Golden Hour Preparation

Swinging a rope in an arena is one discipline; building a loop while navigating rocky desert washes, mesquite, and catclaw is another animal entirely. Your mount must rate the target automatically while you read wind velocity and brush clearance. It is precision horsemanship honed until the loop becomes second nature.

Range Medicine • Two Ropes & Absolute Trust

Pasture Doctoring — Holding the Tension

There are no squeeze chutes on the open range. When an animal needs treatment miles from the pens, you rope and doctor them where they stand. One rider on the head, one on the heels, ropes wrapped taut to the horn, and seasoned horses leaning into their britchen to keep the animal safe and immobile. It demands instinctive teamwork.

Ranch Works • In the Dirt

Branding, Flanking & Processing — The Unvarnished Truth

Ranch life isn't watching sunsets from the saddle; it’s stepping off into the dirt and flanking calves to the ground. In the branding pens, everyone has an assigned role: heeler, flanker, vaccinator, iron man. Down on a knee holding a front leg and neck while the irons smoke and the medicine is administered, you earn every meal.

In the Arena • Timed Precision

Me & Joel — Team Roping

That's me and my partner Joel — Rubio the Palomino under me and Monty the Chestnut under him. Team roping takes open-range mechanics and tests them against the clock. It isn't just competition; it's total reliance on your partner and your horse. Competing since my Queen Creek rodeo days keeps the edge sharp.
